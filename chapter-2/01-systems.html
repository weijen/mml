LESSON 01 / Introduction · §2.1

Equations are constraints

One problem. Three ways to see it.

About 18 minutesBook pp. 17–21 ↗
By the end

You can translate between equations, column combinations, and Ax = b, and explain the three possible solution outcomes.

IN THIS LESSON
START SMALL

One idea for this lesson

Find numbers that make all the equations true.

An equation is like a balance. The left side and the right side must have the same value.

How to read the symbols aloud

Read 2x + y = 7 as: “two times x, plus y, equals seven.”

English help · words used in this lesson
equation
A statement that two amounts are equal.
unknown
A number we have not found yet, such as x.
solution
A value, or a list of values, that makes every equation true.
constraint
A rule that limits the possible answers.

First reading: just these three steps

  1. Write down what x and y mean.
  2. Try the answer in both equations.
  3. Use the diagram to see one answer, no answer, or many answers.

You can stop after these steps and the “Pause & predict” question. Return to the formal details when the main idea feels familiar.

Start with a concrete question

Suppose a workshop makes stools and benches. A stool uses 2 pieces of timber and 1 hour of work; a bench uses 1 piece of timber and 2 hours. You have 7 pieces and 8 hours. How many of each would use both resources exactly?

Let x count stools and y count benches. Each resource supplies one constraint:

2x + y = 7
x + 2y = 8

Try x = 2 and y = 3. The timber equation gives 4 + 3 = 7, and the time equation gives 2 + 6 = 8. A solution must satisfy every equation at once. A pair that satisfies only one constraint is not a solution of the system.

The chapter’s production example uses the same structure with many resources and products. Real production may also need nonnegative or integer quantities; the linear equations alone do not enforce those restrictions.

The row view: intersect constraints

An equation ax + by = c, with at least one of a or b nonzero, describes a line. With three unknowns a nondegenerate equation describes a plane. Solving the system means finding their common intersection.

One solution

Independent constraints meet at one point.

No solution

Constraints contradict each other.

Infinitely many

Some freedom remains after all constraints are satisfied.

The column view: build a target

Read the same workshop problem in a different direction. Each product contributes a bundle of resources. Can you combine the bundles to make the target?

x 21 + y 12 = 78

The first column tells you what one stool contributes. The second tells you what one bench contributes. The unknowns are the weights on those columns. This is the beginning of span: everything you can build from the available columns.

The matrix view: collect the bookkeeping

A x = b   with   A = 2112,   x = xy,   b = 78

In general, A has m rows (equations) and n columns (unknowns). Thus A ∈ ℝm×n, x ∈ ℝn, b ∈ ℝm. The symbol ∈ means “is an element of”; ℝ means the real numbers.

Keep all three interpretations

Rows impose constraints. Columns offer building blocks. The matrix maps an input x to an output Ax. These are three descriptions of the same calculation.

SECOND READING · MORE DETAIL

Why exactly two solutions cannot happen

Suppose distinct vectors p and q both solve Ax = b. By distributing multiplication, every vector p + t(q − p) also solves it:

A[p + t(q − p)] = Ap + t(Aq − Ap) = b + t(b − b) = b

For any real t you get a solution. Since p ≠ q, varying t produces infinitely many distinct points. A real linear system therefore has zero, one, or infinitely many solutions.

Pause & predict

Does having more equations than unknowns guarantee no solution?

Give me a hint

Could an equation repeat information already present?

Show the reasoning

No. For example, x + y = 2, x − y = 0, and 2x + 2y = 4 all hold at (1,1). The third equation is redundant. Consistency and independence matter more than counting equations.
